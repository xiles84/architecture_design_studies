# Analysis — 20260916T090036Z-v3 — claude-opus-5

## TL;DR

- **If the parent row already carries `last_donation_at`, index it and stop there.** D22 —
  D4 plus the single line `CREATE INDEX person_last_donation_global_idx ON person
  (last_donation_at DESC)` — answers "who last gave in this window" at 22,020 ops/s on
  PostgreSQL against D4's own 4,272 without it, and 224x the plain-aggregate floor. It
  needs no new column, no new trigger and no new write path.
- **The owner's flag (D20) is a real answer and a cheap one** — +0.95% storage, 6,219
  inserts/s — but it is 2.9x slower than the indexed rollup on PostgreSQL (7,636 vs
  22,020 ops/s, q13 trailing) and **7.9x slower on YugabyteDB** (219 vs 1,737). Build it
  when there is no parent aggregate to index; prefer the rollup when there is.
- **Take the lock.** D20's person-row lock cost nothing measurable (6,219 vs D21's 5,819
  median inserts/s, inside the run's own spread), while unguarded D21 corrupted 3, 7 and
  4 donors across three trials and 1 more under hot-donor load. A free guard against
  silent wrong answers is not a trade-off.
- **Pessimistic beat optimistic decisively under single-row contention.** At 16 writers
  all hammering one donor, D23's compare-and-set needed 23,468–28,321 retries to land
  4,416–7,764 inserts and had 22–56% of its offered load rejected; D20's row lock served
  100% of the same load with zero retries. The confound is named below.
- **Biggest doubt:** this run never measured a no-flag baseline's write cost, so the
  price of D20's trigger against plain D3 is still unmeasured, and the hot-donor sweep
  was rate-limited below capacity. Both are my specification errors, detailed below.

## What I was looking at

`20260916T090036Z-v3`, digest `1b05f142fd9e06ef`, 192 cells, `small` scale (5,000 donors,
108,081 donations), three independent trials per cell, each on its own fresh load. Four
groups: `recency-reads` (twelve designs × PostgreSQL 1-node and YugabyteDB 1-node),
`recency-maintenance` and `recency-hot-donor` (PostgreSQL only), `recency-placement`
(YugabyteDB 3-node). No analysis existed for this digest.

**Every one of the 192 cells passed its correctness gate, 22 checks each, zero failures** —
including the eight new checks that verify q13–q16 in both window regimes against truth
computed independently in Go. That is the precondition for everything below.

Twenty-two cells are marked "FAILED / diagnostic" in the generated report and excluded
from its rate summaries. I checked each class: four are D21's negative control firing (by
design), nine are D23's operations that the harness deliberately refuses to time (D5's
long-standing rule that its rollups are insert-path only), and nine are cells with write
errors or queue rejections under contention, which are themselves the measurement. **No
correct design failed anything.**

I deliberately ignored the `@historical`-vs-`@trailing` differences below ~1.5x: with
per-cell spreads of 3–47% across three trials on a shared laptop, smaller gaps are not
arguable.

## Conclusions

**The owner's instinct was sound, and the measurement still changes the recommendation.**
A `last_donation` flag on the child, partially indexed, does exactly what they expected: it
turns a question that costs a full aggregate over every donation into one narrow index
range. Against the D3 baseline that derives the answer at read time, D20 is 78x faster on
q13 in the trailing regime (7,636 vs 98 ops/s) and 33x on the lapsed-donor count (6,346 vs
195). It costs +262 KB of the 27.6 MB dataset, under 1%.

But the study's own spine already contained a better answer. D4 and D5 have maintained
`person.last_donation_at` on every write since the original survey; what they never had was
a *global* access path to it, because `person_last_donation_idx` leads with `charity_id`.
One index line closes that gap. D22 reaches 22,020 ops/s on q13 trailing where D4 manages
4,272 — **a 5.2x gain from one line and zero new maintenance** — and beats the flag by 2.9x.
On YugabyteDB the margin widens to 7.9x (1,737 vs 219). The two tie on the lapsed count
(5,663 vs 6,346, inside the noise), which fits: that question returns most of the donor
base, so both designs end up scanning most of an index either way.

The practical rule that falls out: **ask where the fact already lives before materialising
it somewhere new.** A team that already keeps a per-donor rollup should add an index. A team
that does not should weigh D20's flag against adding the rollup column itself — and this run
cannot settle that second comparison, because D22 carries D4's *entire* rollup package (five
columns maintained on both `person` and `charity`), not just `last_donation_at`. That is why
D22 inserts at 3,436/s against D20's 6,219/s: the gap prices four extra maintained columns
and a second hot parent row, not "rollup vs flag". **D20 → D22 is a clean one-decision pair
on the read side and a confounded one on the write side**, and I should have caught that when
I specified it. The missing design is a `last_donation_at`-only rollup — the recency analogue
of D16's sum-only trigger.

**The guard is free.** D20 and D21 differ by exactly one statement, a `SELECT … FOR UPDATE`
on the donor's `person` row. Their insert throughput is indistinguishable: medians 6,219 and
5,819, with D21's three trials at 1,278 / 6,039 / 5,819 — a spread wide enough that its own
outlier exceeds the difference between the designs. Meanwhile D21 left 4, 7 and 3 donors with
a wrong or duplicated flag in the three maintenance trials, and 1 more at 16 hot-donor
writers. A design that is no faster and sometimes wrong has nothing to recommend it. This is
also the clearest demonstration yet that the recency audit works: it caught corruption the
throughput numbers were entirely happy with.

**Optimistic concurrency collapsed where pessimistic did not.** The hot-donor sweep points
every writer at a single donor. At 16 writers, D23 — the application-maintained rollup using
compare-and-set — burned 23,468–28,321 retries to complete 4,416–7,764 inserts, exhausted its
50-attempt limit 11–18 times per cell, and had 2,225–5,569 requests rejected by the queue
because workers could not drain it. D20 and D21, which take a row lock, completed all 10,000
at zero retries and zero rejections. Two caveats keep this from being a clean
optimistic-vs-pessimistic verdict: D23 maintains far more state per transaction than D20's
one boolean, and its retry loop wraps the whole transaction rather than one statement. What
the numbers do support without qualification is narrower and still useful: **under maximal
same-row contention, a CAS loop over a multi-statement rollup transaction degrades
catastrophically, and a single row lock does not.**

**Embedding buys nothing here, as the protocol predicted before the run.** D6, which holds
the whole history in the donor row, answers q13 at 19.9 ops/s — five times *slower* than the
plain aggregate it was supposed to beat, because every donor's document must be unnested to
find its maximum. D9 and D10, whose bounded cache holds the newest 20 newest-first so element
0 *is* the answer, land at 196–201 — statistically the same as D3's 98–177 derive, because
there is no index on a JSON expression to make that head seekable. Prediction and measurement
agree: **for a cross-parent recency question, embedding pays its write cost and buys nothing.**

**The genuine surprise is D18.** The parent-driven probe — walk `person`, take each donor's
maximum with one descent of an index that already exists — is the formulation an experienced
DBA might reach for first. It is *slower than the naive aggregate* on PostgreSQL (82 vs 98
ops/s on q13 trailing) and it falls off a cliff on YugabyteDB: **0.92 ops/s, 22x slower than
the same engine's plain `GROUP BY`**. 5,000 index descents beat one sequential scan and hash
aggregate at this scale, and on a distributed store each descent is an RPC. D19's window-first
rewrite is the formulation that actually pays — 3,231 ops/s on q14 trailing against 699 in the
historical regime, a 4.6x swing that is the regime distinction doing exactly what RECENCY.md
said it would: the anti-join is trivially empty when the window ends at the dataset's edge and
does real work when it does not.

**Colocation is a null result.** D20 against D24 on the three-node cluster differs by 1.5–20%
on every question and on inserts (134 vs 152/s), with spreads of 9.9–54%. Nothing here is
arguable. This makes sense mechanically — these are cross-donor questions served from an index
spanning all donors, so per-donor tablet locality has nothing to offer them — and it is worth
recording precisely because the opposite is often assumed.

## Where I think the measurement is weak

- **No no-flag write baseline.** `recency-maintenance` covers only D20–D23. Nothing in this
  run measures D3's insert throughput under the same image, scale and conditions, so **the
  flag's maintenance cost against not having it is unmeasured**. Comparing to a v3 run would
  cross images and dates, which this repository rightly treats as invalid. My specification
  error; the group should have included D3.
- **The hot-donor sweep never reached capacity.** Every healthy cell completed exactly 10,000
  at 500.0/s — the offered arrival rate — at every writer count from 1 to 16. The experiment
  therefore measures "who keeps up with 500/s", not peak throughput, and the writer count
  varies concurrency while the offered load stays fixed. The D23 collapse is real because it
  is a *failure to keep up*; the absence of differences among the others says nothing about
  their ceilings. The rate should scale with the writer count, or sit well above capacity.
- **Three trials, 3–47% spread.** Anything under ~1.5x in this run is noise. Two cells
  (D22 hot-donor w1 t2 and w16 t2) lost 34–43% of their offered load with zero retries and
  zero errors, which does not match any mechanism in that design; I read them as trial noise
  on a shared laptop, not a property of D22, but I cannot prove that from three trials.
- **YugabyteDB numbers remain quota-bound** on this host, as every study-01 and study-02
  result has been. Cross-engine ratios are indicative, not portable.
- **Single scale.** Everything above is `small`. The flag's partial index stays one row per
  donor by construction, but the *relative* cost of an aggregate scan against an index seek
  grows with history length, so the 224x gap over the derive floor should widen at `medium`
  and narrow toward 1 for a dataset where most donors have a single donation.
- **D21's clean audits under hot-donor load prove little.** The control fired in only one of
  twelve hot-donor cells. Per the phase-1 lesson, a paced open-loop arrival experiment is a
  gentler race than a closed-loop one; the absence of firing at 1, 4 and 8 writers reflects
  the load shape, not safety.

## What I would measure next

1. **D25, a `last_donation_at`-only rollup** — D3 plus that one column, maintained by a
   trigger that touches nothing else, plus the global index. This is the missing rung that
   turns D20 → D22 into a real one-decision write-side pair, and it is the design I would
   actually recommend to someone without an existing rollup.
2. **Add D3 to `recency-maintenance`** and re-run that group. One line in the runner; it
   prices every flag and rollup insert cost against not having one.
3. **Re-run `recency-hot-donor` with the arrival rate scaled to the writer count** (or with
   a closed-loop writer pool), so the sweep measures capacity rather than compliance. D23's
   ceiling under CAS is the number this study is missing.
4. **`recency-reads` at `medium`** for D3, D18, D19, D20, D22 only, to test whether the
   derive-versus-materialise gap widens with history as predicted.
5. **A partial-index-versus-full-index pair for D20** (`WHERE is_last_donation` against a
   plain `(is_last_donation, donated_at DESC)`), to price the partial index's narrowness
   separately from the flag itself — and to check whether YugabyteDB plans it differently,
   since D20's regime sensitivity there (219 trailing vs 902 historical, a 4.1x swing
   PostgreSQL does not show) is unexplained by anything in this run.
