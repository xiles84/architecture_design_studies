## Conclusions

### 1. The answers to the questions the study was built to answer

On PostgreSQL, single node. Throughputs are reads/s from the survey unless stated.

| Question | Use | Evidence | Instead of |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Who donates the most** | **Rollup on the parent (D4)** | D3 563 → D4 **34k** (60x). Plan: 1 220 buffers → **3** | aggregating donations at read time |
| **Total donated (global)** | **Rollup (D4)** | D3 286 → D4 **34k** (119x) | `SUM` over every donation |
| **Total donated (one charity)** | Rollup (D4), or a covering index if you won't maintain rollups | D2 397 → D3 covering index 2.5k (6x) → D4 36k | a join through `person` |
| **Last donation (global or per charity)** | **An index on `donated_at`** — a rollup does not beat it | D2 39k, D3 40k, D4 27k | a rollup column |
| **Who donated last** | Index with the grandparent key denormalised (D3) | D2 19k → D3 22k; D4 25k (tie) | — |
| **First and last donation of a person** | **Composite index `(person_id, donated_at)`** | D2 34k, D4 37k (tie) | a rollup column |
| **How many donations a person made** | Composite index | D2 38k, D4 37k (tie) | a rollup counter |
| A donor's 20 most recent donations | Composite index on PostgreSQL | D3 32k vs D9 cache 27k (`history-unbounded`) | an embedded cache |
| Charity activity feed | Denormalise `charity_id` onto `donation` (D3) | D2 1.3k → D3 **12k** (9x) | a join through `person` |

**The shape of the answer:** of the owner's five questions, only the *aggregate* ones —
"who donates the most" and "how much in total" — justify a rollup. Every *navigational* or
*extreme-value* question ("last", "first", "count") is already answered at 30–40k/s by a
well-chosen composite index, and a rollup adds write cost without adding read speed there.

### 2. Design verdicts in the general case

The general case is unbounded history, 10 large charities, mixed traffic.

- **D1 (no secondary indexes) — never.** 13.3x worse read score than D2 for inserts that are
  within noise of each other (6.2k vs 6.7k). Even on the `tiny` dataset (11k donations),
  indexes still buy **3.1x** (`size-tiny`). Its limitation never becomes acceptable in any
  regime measured. The claim that indexes "cost writes" was not measurable on PostgreSQL at
  this scale; it was on YugabyteDB 3-node (inserts 0.66x), where each index is a
  distributed table.
- **D2 → D3 (denormalise the grandparent key) — yes.** Removes a join from six queries;
  charity feed 9x, charity total 6x via a covering index. Cost: +45% storage (18.1 → 26.3
  MiB) and inserts 0.89x (inside the error bar). The unenforced invariant is the real cost:
  moving a donor between charities makes every donation row lie.
- **D3 → D8 (drop foreign keys) — no measurable gain; keep them.** With 3 trials on fresh
  loads: PostgreSQL inserts 6.7k (FK) vs 6.3k (no FK); YugabyteDB 3-node 384 vs 321. The
  survey's "no-FK deletes 2x slower" was noise and phase-order bias, and disappeared when
  re-measured. See weakness W5 for the one unresolved YugabyteDB 1-node figure.
- **D4 (trigger rollups) — the best design for aggregates, with a sharp operating limit.**
  Read score 3.6–3.9x D3, and 60–130x on the aggregate questions. Inserts cost **2.4x**
  (2.8k vs 6.7k, trials). Corrections 2.5x, single removals 5.6x, and **erasure 14x** (389 vs
  5.6k), because a MIN/MAX cannot be decremented and every child delete recomputes the
  parent. Storage is actually *lower* than D3 (23.2 vs 26.3 MiB), because a rollup makes the
  covering index unnecessary. See §4 for the concurrency limit that decides whether it is
  usable.
- **D5 (application-maintained rollups) — prefer the trigger.** Same reads, slower writes at
  every concurrency level measured (1 writer: 1.2k vs 1.8k; 8 writers: 2.3k vs 3.0k),
  because the application spends three round trips on what the trigger does in-process.
  Its one advantage — explicit `FOR UPDATE` — only shows at 32 writers (1.7k vs 1.1k, at the
  edge of the error bar).
- **D6 (embed all donations as JSONB) — not in the general case.** Read score **0.12x** D3.
  Cross-donor questions collapse by 30–1 100x (global last donation 35.9/s vs 40k/s).
  Correcting an amount is 16x slower (418 vs 6.7k), removing one donation 16x (457 vs 7.3k).
  Two measured surprises cut the other way: **a donor editing their profile is not slower**
  (7.2k vs 6.7k — the "rewrite the whole row" worry did not materialise), and **erasing a
  donor is the fastest of any design** (7.4k vs D3 5.6k; D4 389). See §3.
- **D9 (bounded embedded cache) — not worth it on single-node PostgreSQL.** It costs 0.78x
  on inserts and +13% storage, and the query it exists for is *slower* than the index it
  duplicates (27k vs 32k/s). **As first written, it also corrupted data** (§5). Its case is
  a distributed store (§3).
- **D10 (D9 with a correct trigger) — correctness is free.** Zero wrong caches in 4
  contended windows plus 5 isolated write ops, at 0.96–0.98x D9's write throughput (inside
  the per-cell spread). If a D9-style cache is used at all, it must be D10's trigger.
- **D7 (YugabyteDB: shard donations by `person_id`) — a good default on YugabyteDB.**
  Inserts **1.5x** D3 on both 1- and 3-node clusters (453 vs 300; 239 vs 156), with reads
  within noise. "A donor's recent donations" is 2x D3 on 3 nodes (3.9k vs 2.0k). The
  primary key already orders a donor's donations, so one fewer distributed index is written.

### 3. Regime notes — when a design that loses in general is the right one

The owner asked for these explicitly, and they are the part of this analysis most likely to
change a real decision. Each note says what regime was measured and what it showed.

> **Note R1 — Rollups (D4) are the best overall design when parents are many and small
> relative to write concurrency.**
> With 1 000 small charities instead of 10 large ones (same people, same donations), D4's
> insert cost falls from **0.46x to 0.90x** of D3, while it keeps a **1.7x** read advantage
> (`top-1000charities`). At 32 concurrent writers D4 goes from **951 → 6 200 inserts/s**,
> scaling *positively* instead of collapsing (`contention-{10,1000}charities`). With few,
> large parents, D4 is still fine at **≤ 4 concurrent writers** (experiment C: 3.4k at 4
> writers, then 3.0k → 1.7k → 1.1k as writers grow). **Avoid D4 when a handful of parent
> rows absorb most writes from many concurrent writers.** In that case, compute aggregates
> at read time with a covering index, or maintain them asynchronously (not measured here).

> **Note R2 — Embedding (D6) reaches parity only for per-donor workloads, and it never wins
> outright on PostgreSQL.**
> Bounding history to 20 donations per donor fixes D6's *write* amplification — corrections
> 3.2x faster, inserts 0.62x → 0.78x of D3 — but **not** its reads (read score 866 → 931).
> Total volume is unchanged, so cross-donor questions still unnest every element. On a
> donor-portal workload that never crosses donors, D6 reads at **0.79x** D3 with unbounded
> history and **0.90x** with bounded history (a tie by the error bar) (`portal-*`).
> **Choose D6 only if all three hold:** reads are per-donor, history is naturally bounded,
> and cross-donor questions are served from somewhere else. Erasure-heavy workloads (GDPR
> deletion at volume) are a secondary argument: D6 erases fastest, though only a tie
> against D3.

> **Note R3 — An embedded cache (D9/D10) pays off on a distributed store, not on
> single-node PostgreSQL.**
> On PostgreSQL the composite index already serves "a donor's recent donations" faster than
> the cache (32k vs 27k). On YugabyteDB 3-node the cache is **2x** D3 (4.0k vs 2.0k), because
> reading children through a secondary index there costs cross-node RPCs. **On YugabyteDB,
> prefer D7 over D9/D10:** placement gives the same 2x (3.9k) with *faster* inserts, no
> trigger to get wrong, and no second copy of the data.

> **Note R4 — No regime makes an unindexed schema (D1) acceptable.** Indexing still paid
> 3.1x at 11 000 donations.

> **Note R5 — No measured regime gives dropping foreign keys (D8) a performance reason.**
> Its acceptable case is organisational: a single writer that owns the invariant. That is not
> something a benchmark can establish.

> **Note R6 — Application-maintained rollups (D5) are acceptable for append-only ledgers.**
> Its write path maintains aggregates on insert only. It was measured, and correct, only
> for that. Any workload with corrections or refunds needs D4's recompute logic.

### 4. Concurrency control and isolation (experiment C, PostgreSQL)

- **No strategy lost an update**, at any isolation level. Every rollup increment is a
  single `UPDATE` executed under the engine's row lock. That protection does *not* extend
  to a read-then-write split across statements, which none of these strategies used.
- **Pessimistic beats optimistic under contention.** At 32 writers on 10 charities,
  `FOR UPDATE` held 1.7k/s while optimistic version checks fell to **534/s** (retry storms).
  At low contention they are equal.
- **Serializable costs ~7x and fails transactions outright.** 2.3k/s at read committed vs
  324/s at serializable, with 33 000 retries and **28 transactions that still failed** —
  application-visible failures. The harness does not record whether those exhausted their
  50 retries or hit a non-retryable error. It bought nothing: read committed was already
  correct for these statements.
- **Every design keeps its read advantage under concurrent writes, but not uniformly**
  (experiment D, 4 readers : 4 writers). D4 keeps only **45%** of its read throughput, the
  largest loss, because its writers are the most expensive. That is still 13k/s against
  D3's 3.0k/s.

### 5. Correctness findings — the most important results in the study

1. **A trigger-maintained cache (D9) silently corrupted 3–5 donor caches per 30-second
   window** under concurrent writes (`d9-cache-race`), rising with writer count. Two
   independent bugs, both confirmed from recorded examples:
   - **ordering race:** the cache was in commit order, the table in timestamp order;
   - **lost update:** a rebuild's `UPDATE` waited on a lock, then wrote a cache computed
     from a snapshot taken *before* the competing insert committed.

   It passed the survey's audit only because the audit ran once, at the end of the cell,
   after later phases had rebuilt and **healed** most caches. **D10 fixes both at no
   measurable cost** (`cache-fix`, `cache-fix-writes`).
   **General lesson:** "the database handles concurrency" holds for a statement's *target
   row*, not for everything that statement reads.
2. **Consolidated rollups (D4, D5) stayed exactly correct** in every audit, under every
   strategy, isolation level and write mix.

### 6. Engine and topology

- **PostgreSQL is ~12x faster on reads and ~18x on inserts than YugabyteDB 1-node** at this
  scale and per-node budget (D3: 8.1k vs 641 reads; 5.5k vs 300 inserts). This is the
  expected cost of a distributed storage engine on a dataset that fits one node. It is not
  a verdict on YugabyteDB, which is built for failure tolerance and for data that does not
  fit one machine — neither of which this study measured.
- **Adding two nodes (RF=3) lowered write throughput and did not raise reads.** Inserts
  0.52x (D3) to 0.34x (D4) of 1-node; read scores flat, between 0.74x and 1.24x. RF=3 means
  every write reaches consensus on three nodes. What you get for two more machines here is
  fault tolerance, not speed. That changes when data outgrows one node, which `small` never
  does.
- **Rollups suffer most from distribution** (D4 inserts 163 → 55). Parent and child updates
  become distributed transactions spanning tablets.

## Where I think the measurement is weak

- **W1 — One laptop.** Client and databases share 8 heterogeneous cores. There is no real
  network, so the 3-node penalties in §6 are *understated*: real cross-zone consensus costs
  milliseconds, not microseconds.
- **W2 — Small data only.** ~108 000 donations fit in memory, so no result here says
  anything about I/O-bound behaviour. I expect D1's and D6's penalties to grow with scale,
  and D4's advantage to grow with it. That is an expectation, not a finding — `medium` was
  never run.
- **W3 — Mostly single trials.** The survey, experiment C, experiment D and all regime runs
  are single-trial. They are used here only for differences of 1.5x or more. Only
  `writes-isolated` and `cache-fix-writes` are 3-trial.
- **W4 — Tail latencies are optimistic.** The harness is closed-loop, so coordinated
  omission biases p99.9+ low. Tails here are only compared between designs.
- **W5 — One unreplicated FK figure.** The YugabyteDB 1-node survey showed FKs costing
  1.8x on inserts (300 vs 548). The 3-trial re-run was done on 3-node only, where the gap
  vanished. The 1-node figure was neither confirmed nor refuted.
- **W6 — Binaries changed between passes.** Several fixes landed mid-study: finite-pool
  deletes, per-op reload, representative EXPLAIN keys, deep tails. Each run's manifest
  records its image id. Survey cells and later runs were not all produced by the same binary.
- **W7 — The workload mixes are my invention.** The dashboard and portal weights are
  plausible, not observed from a real charity system. Experiment D's retention percentages
  depend on them.
- **W8 — I built what I am judging.** See the note at the top.
