## TL;DR

- **Make the sale a fact, not a state, before promising the business a refund report.**
  r05 is `unanswerable` in 14 of the 15 designs and r01/r03 are `partial` in those same 14,
  because cancellation deletes the sale or nulls its buyer. X1's append-only `sale_event`
  row, written in the same statement, is the only design that answers all three correctly
  (r05 32.0k ops/s, r01 32.4k, r03 176 on PostgreSQL).
- **The report that decides the architecture is r03, "customers whose last purchase falls
  in a window", not the one that sounds expensive.** It runs at 134–232 ops/s on
  PostgreSQL against 32k ops/s for "a customer's tickets" — two orders of magnitude — and
  at 5.3–50 ops/s on YugabyteDB. Any design choice that adds a per-customer sort to the
  reporting path exposes it again.
- **On YugabyteDB the ledger is worth 5.6–5.9x on exactly that report** (5.3 → 29.9 ops/s
  on `yb-single`; 7.2 → 42.2 on `yb-cluster3`). On PostgreSQL the same change is inside
  the noise (167 → 176). The ledger's value is engine-dependent, and it is largest where
  per-row reads are most expensive.
- **The designs that were wrong for the drop are the fastest at the management report.**
  Sell-through per band runs at 5.5k ops/s on C1 — the negative control — against 1.7k on
  P3 and X1, because a design with no per-seat rows has no per-seat aggregate to compute.
  Do not let a fast report table pick the inventory model.
- **Do not read the P3 → X1 read rows as the ledger's read cost.** X1's buyer reads came
  out ~2x slower across the board, including a primary-key lookup whose recorded plan is
  identical (Index Scan on `ticket_pkey`, `shared hit=4`), so the difference is not the
  ledger; it is a cell-level effect. The controlled pair's write axis is what AM-04.2
  says it is, and that is measured in the race runs, not here.

## Conclusions

**1. Answerability, not throughput, is the result.** The answerability table is the part
of this report a reader should act on. Every one of the fourteen designs that stores
inventory as seat state loses the sale when the sale is undone: `w_cancel_ticket` sets the
row back to `available` and nulls `customer_id`/`sold_at`, or deletes the row. So "how
much did we refund in this window" has no answer at all in 14 of 15 designs, and
"sales and revenue in a window" (r01) and "customers whose last purchase falls in a
window" (r03) are marked `partial` in those same 14 because a refunded purchase
disappears or is dated by an earlier one. This is not a performance finding that tuning
can address; it is a data-model finding. The revenue number a finance team reads off P3
is wrong across a refund, and no index changes that.

**2. X1 buys correctness and, on some engines, speed.** With `sale_event`, r05 becomes
answerable (32.0k ops/s trailing, 27.0k historical on `pg-single`; 3.4k on `yb-single`;
4.3k on `yb-cluster3`) and r01 becomes the *correct* gross figure at 32.4k ops/s against
P3's 10k — 3.2x faster, in addition to being right. r03 gains 1.05x on PostgreSQL
(167 → 176, inside the run's noise) but **5.6x on `yb-single` (5.3 → 29.9) and 5.9x on
`yb-cluster3` (7.2 → 42.2)**, because the ledger can be pruned by `at` per customer while
the ticket-table formulation scans per-design state. If the deployment target is
YugabyteDB, this is the strongest single argument for the ledger in the run.

**3. r03 is the report that costs, everywhere, whatever the design.** On PostgreSQL the
range is 134 ops/s (P1) to 232 (C1); on `yb-single` it is 5.6 (P1) to 41.6 (C4); on
`yb-cluster3` 7.0 to 50.1. Against the buyer's own reads in the same cells — 32k for "a
customer's tickets", 39k for "ticket by id" — that is 100–600x on PostgreSQL and 100–800x
on YugabyteDB. The mechanism is visible in the plans: it is the only report that has to
establish, per customer, which of their purchases is the latest inside a window, and no
design has an index that makes that a single ordered lookup. The practical consequence is
that this report cannot be run synchronously on a request path in any of these designs;
it belongs in a background job, and designing it as a report rather than a query is the
next decision.

**4. Aggregate reports favour designs that cannot sell seats carefully.** r04
(sell-through per event, by band) is the cheapest report in the matrix on the counting
and inventory designs — C1 5.5k, C3 5.7k, C2 5.3k ops/s on PostgreSQL — and 1.6–1.7k on
P3 and X1. The reason is structural: a design without per-seat rows (C family) computes
capacity from a stored counter on the event row; a design with per-seat rows has to
aggregate them (P3, X1, R3, and the H family at 1.7k). C1 is in this study *because it
overbooks*. A reader scanning the report's throughput tables from the top would conclude
the wrong design is the fast one; the answerability column and the study 02 v1 analysis
are what prevent that misreading, and this run's r04 column should never be quoted
without them.

**5. The ledger's visible cost in this run is state, not reads.** On `pg-single` X1's
loader total is 6.4 s against P3's 0.8 s and its storage 62.6 MiB against 46.3 MiB
(+35 %); on `yb-single` 32.4 s against 18.9 s. Those are load-and-storage numbers for a
second append-only table plus its four indexes, and they are the price of answerability
paid once per load, not per sale. Whether the same ledger costs the *selling* path is the
question 3b-2/3b-3 were run to answer, and this file deliberately does not answer it.

**6. The P3 → X1 read rows in the controlled-pair section are not attributable.**
Every buyer-read row for X1 came out slower than P3's: 9.1k vs 17k (seats left, 10-seat
event), 204 vs 619 (100k-seat event), 14k vs 32k (a customer's tickets), 20k vs 39k
(ticket by id). Ticket-by-id is a primary-key lookup: `sale_event` cannot mechanically
affect it. The captured plans agree — both are `Index Scan using ticket_pkey`, both
`Buffers: shared hit=4` — while the plan's own execution time doubles with the
throughput (0.011 ms → 0.021 ms), i.e. the whole cell was slower per operation, not one
query. The report's error bar is computed from C1/C2/C3, which read byte-identical SQL
*inside one cell* (81 comparisons, median 1.04x, worst 2.03x), so it cannot detect a
difference between two different cells' loads. The conclusion I draw is that this run
measures the *reports* matrix well and the *P3 → X1 read axis* not at all; the pair's
write axis, which AM-04.2 defines precisely, is the one the race runs measure.

## Where I think the measurement is weak

- **No contention evidence in this run.** The phases are `verify,explain,read`, so
  neither negative control fired — the report says so itself ("did not fire — this
  experiment did not generate enough contention"). Every "none observed" row therefore
  means "not exercised here". The gate's 46/46 checks (48/48 for X1) do pass on the
  loaded state, which is worth having, but they are answer checks, not concurrency
  evidence. This is the single weakest thing about this digest.
- **The databases are quota-bound in most periods.** On `pg-single` the database
  container was throttled in 1803 of 1835 CFS periods for P1 (681 s of paused time) and
  in no less than 70 % of them for any design; on `yb-single` ~90 % (1942/2161 for P3);
  on `yb-cluster3` the ratios read lower (1825–2197 of ~5800–9075) only because the
  counter sums periods across three nodes, i.e. ~28–38 % of node-periods are still
  throttled. The designs whose *client* is throttled hardest (P4, C4, R1 — 50–58 s of the
  read phase) are the ones whose database is throttled least, which is what a fixed total
  core budget looks like. Absolute ops/s here are a property of `--cpus 2`, not of the
  engines, and cross-topology comparisons carry that asymmetry.
- **One trial, 10 s per measurement, closed loop, 8 connections, shared laptop cores.**
  Differences below the 2.03x worst control disagreement are not attributable; the r03
  ordering between P1/P2/P3 (134/166/167) is inside that band and I do not rely on it.
- **The r03 numbers are so low that client-side effects could dominate.** At 134 ops/s
  with 8 workers the client is doing very little, so the column is measuring database
  work, but no client-CPU reading isolates it; a single-worker repeat would settle it.
- **r06 is answerable in only two designs** (H0/H1), so the "outstanding holds" row is a
  two-column comparison and not a design study.
- **The ledger's hot path is unmeasured here** by construction; the X1 load total (6.4 s)
  is a loader artefact and must not be quoted as the cost of the ledger under demand.

## What I would measure next

1. `run-study.sh --designs p3_precreated_cas,x1_cas_ledger --phases verify,read
   --trials 3` **with the design order reversed and interleaved between trials** — the
   cheap test of the ~2x cell effect this analysis found. If the gap follows the order,
   it is not the ledger.
2. A refund-heavy r01/r05 workload: churn the loaded tickets, then run the reports, so
   the partial/answerable distinction is measured with refunds present rather than
   inferred from the schema. This is also where C1/H0 must be re-run to fire.
3. The protocol's own gap, §6: the reports run *concurrently* with a sell-out race, at
   32 and 128 buyers, answering "does the finance query throttle the drop".
4. A C4-ledger variant (`c4_counter` + `sale_event`) to separate "answering reports from
   a ledger" from "having per-seat rows at all" — C4's r04 4.8k against X1's 1.7k says
   these are different costs and the study so far has them confounded by P3's seat rows.
5. Long-horizon ledger growth: this ledger is as old as one run; the `yb-cluster3`
   append point has never been measured under months of history.
