## Conclusions

**1. This is the run to quote, and the sibling is the reason to be careful.** Both runs
measure the same pair; only the buyer count and the design order differ. On YugabyteDB they
agree: X1 costs about 2x the race (this run 1.8–2.2x, sibling 1.7–5.7x) and about 2.4x the
churn throughput. On PostgreSQL they disagree in *sign*: the 32-buyer run has X1 faster
(but with P3 trial spreads of 92–283 %), this 128-buyer run has P3 faster with 4–37 %
spreads. The defensible reading is that **the ledger costs roughly 2x a hot drop on
YugabyteDB, and between 1x and 2.3x on PostgreSQL depending on how hard the drop is
pushed** — and that the 128-buyer run is the better-conditioned of the two, because its
trials agree and the contention is closer to a real drop. Anyone quoting a PostgreSQL
ratio from the 32-buyer arm alone is quoting a median whose own trials disagree by more
than the difference.

**2. Where the cost comes from is now visible in the counters.** The ledger does not change
how many attempts a sale needs (13.07 against 13.16 at the 10-seat tier, 1.19 against 1.20
at 10k) — it changes how long each attempt takes. On a contended YugabyteDB node, p99 sale
latency doubles (471→1382 ms at 10 seats) and the last buyer's "sold out, sorry" waits
longer (289→553 ms at 10 seats, 3703→6991 ms at 10k). That is the signature of one more
round trip and one more row's worth of work inside a transaction that is already being
retried by a crowd — which is exactly what study 02's v1 analysis identified as
YugabyteDB's binding constraint here.

**3. Outside the race, the refund is the operation to watch.** Book throughput falls 1.4x–1.5x
on YugabyteDB, but cancel falls 2.1x–2.5x, and cancel is where AM-04.1's order-free
attribution lives: the refund takes a `FOR UPDATE` read of the ticket row so the ledger can
name the buyer being refunded, then inserts. AM-04.2's statement of the pair — "the refund
path is P3's, plus one ledger insert and the locking read that insert needs" — is confirmed
by that ratio and by nothing else in the run being that expensive. On PostgreSQL the same
pair is 4.6k against 5.2k cancels/s, i.e. the added read is lost in the noise; the cost is
engine-specific and should be quoted per engine or not at all.

**4. Correctness held where it matters.** The churn race is the setting that separates
"lost a sale" from "sold the same seat twice": every cell reports 0 under-booked seats
after the sweep, and cancels/swept-up counts per design differ by a few percent at most
(e.g. `yb-cluster3` 10 seats: 148 cancels P3, 159 X1, 0 under-booked both). With the gate
at 48/48 for X1 and its ledger audits clean after every tier on every topology, X1 is
correct under the contention shape that produced the study's original ordering bug — the
thing AM-04 was written to fix, verified by the run it was written for.

**5. A ledger row is not a local change to the hot path.** The organiser's edit — an update
to the event page, touching neither tickets nor the ledger — is 5x slower at p99 with X1 on
PostgreSQL (65→69 ms at 1k seats is not the point; 66→99 ms at 100k and 19→43 ms at 100
seats are). Both designs share the same cores and the same WAL, and the extra write stream
is visible to an unrelated statement. Any design review that prices an added write per sale
should price it against every other writer, not just against its own transaction.

## Where I think the measurement is weak

- **No negative control exists in this run either.** Only P3 and X1 were measured, so the
  report's control line is "0 of 0 fired"; the audit's power to catch overbooking under this
  contention is inherited from the study's published matrix and the AM-02/AM-03 dev checks,
  not demonstrated here. The clean audits are strong evidence of X1's correctness, weak
  evidence about the audit's sensitivity.
- **The two arms of the pair disagree on PostgreSQL and I have not proven why.** Buyer count
  and design order changed together, so this pair cannot separate them; the first cell of a
  topology is also the one whose host state is least controlled (the laptop's other work,
  the previous topology's teardown, WSL memory reclaim).
- **One trial per cell in the design-level sense.** The 3-trial spreads are within a cell;
  there is no repetition of a whole cell, so a systematic per-cell effect — which is
  exactly what the PostgreSQL disagreement looks like — is invisible to these spreads.
- **The database was throttled for much of every cell** (PostgreSQL `pg-single`: 638/1000
  periods for P3, 1293/1762 for X1; `yb-cluster3`: 7822/29687 and 13168/83280 — the
  denominators differ because X1's cell runs longer). Absolute sold/s are quota-bound; only
  the ratios between designs measured under the same quota are argued here.
- **The 100k tier timed out on both YugabyteDB topologies for both designs** (P3 31 % sold,
  X1 15–16 %), so the largest drop is the least measured.
- **Closed-loop buyers, shared cores, no network between the three nodes.** The 3-node
  numbers are a placement experiment, not a distributed-systems one.
- **Wall clock drifted above the plan**: 2 h 17 min against the calibration's ~1.7 h, because
  128-buyer tiers on a throttled YugabyteDB take far longer than the 32-buyer calibration
  cells. It cost time, not validity; the 10 h guard was applied before the runs (Decision
  Log D7 in `docs/handoffs/20260915-recency-and-reports/PROGRESS.md`).

## What I would measure next

1. **Separate order from buyer count**: repeat both arms at 32 and 128 buyers with the
   orders swapped, or better, interleave the two designs tier-by-tier inside one run so a
   within-run control exists. This is the one experiment that would convert conclusion 1
   from "between 1x and 2.3x" into a number.
2. **Controls in the race run**: add `c1_count_naive` so the audit is seen to catch
   overbooking in the regime whose correctness is claimed.
3. **Refund-heavy drop**: raise churn to 50 % and 100 % on `yb-single` and measure the
   sweep-up and cancel latency, not just throughput — the funnel a real box office sees.
4. **The organiser edit under load**: make the unrelated update a first-class measurement
   at 1/10/100 writers, since the ledger's effect on it was larger than on any report.
5. **A `medium` scale drop**: every number here is `small` (289 500 seats); the ledger's
   cost may fall as the ticket table grows relative to the ledger.
