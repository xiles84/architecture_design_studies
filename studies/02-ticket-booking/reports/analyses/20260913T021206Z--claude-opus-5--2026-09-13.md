# Analysis — study 02, run 20260913T021206Z — claude-opus-5

## TL;DR

- **Every design meant to be correct held the invariant; both deliberately wrong ones broke
  it on all three topologies.** No overbooking in 12 designs × 3 topologies; C1 overbooked
  186–189 events and H0 4–18 events per topology. The audit is shown to work, so "no
  violations" means something here. (34 cells of correct designs: 12 × 3 topologies, less
  C2's two failed YugabyteDB cells.)
- **Pre-creating seats is the fast path for a sell-out, not the slow one** — if the seat is
  taken without serialising buyers. On PostgreSQL, P3 (compare-and-set) sold 5.9–6.9k seats/s
  and P2/R3 (SKIP LOCKED) 3.4–4.3k/s on events of 100 seats and more, against ~830–900/s for
  every design that funnels buyers through one counter row (C4, R1, P4). Its price is paid at
  publishing: 404–640 ms (PostgreSQL) and 1.6–9.6 s (YugabyteDB) to publish a 100 000-seat
  event, versus 1–34 ms (mostly under 8 ms) without per-seat rows.
- **Put a counter on the event row and the organiser pays too.** C4 → R1 did not change sales
  speed, but moving the counter to its own row cut the p99 of an unrelated edit to the event
  page from 155–174 ms to 2–14 ms on PostgreSQL and from 2.0–2.4 s to 23–62 ms on YugabyteDB.
- **Avoid two designs that look reasonable:** `max(seat)+1` with a unique index (C5) under-sold
  after refunds on every topology (27–148 seats), and SERIALIZABLE check-then-insert (C2) sold
  116–242 seats/s on PostgreSQL and **took the YugabyteDB SQL layer down** on both YugabyteDB
  topologies.
- **Holds must be validated when confirming, in the same statement, by the database clock.**
  The naive confirmation (H0) oversold 26 events across the run; the checked one (H1)
  rejected 6–468 late confirmations per event size instead, and never oversold.
- **Biggest doubt:** every database container was CPU-throttled for most of the run, one
  trial was taken, and the harness retries without backoff. The last point probably
  understates R2 (buckets) and the SKIP LOCKED designs on small events — see below.

## Conclusions

### 1. The invariant: what actually protects it

Nine different mechanisms held capacity in every cell where they ran: a locked seat row (P1),
a skipped-lock seat row (P2, P4), compare-and-set on the seat row (P3), a serializable
transaction (C2, on PostgreSQL), a locked parent row (C3), a guarded counter (C4, R1), bucketed
counters (R2), a pool of seat tokens (R3), and a confirmation conditioned on hold validity (H1).
The report's gate shows no overbooked event, no duplicate seat, no counter drift and no ledger
mismatch for any of them, after every writing phase.

The two controls show the failures are real and not rare under this workload: C1 (check, then
insert, at READ COMMITTED) overbooked **every** race event it finished on PostgreSQL — 100 of
100 ten-seat events, 20/20, 5/5, 2/2 — with +2 276 extra seats on the ten-seat tier alone. It
did not overbook the 100 000-seat event only because that race timed out at 52% sold: the race
window only matters near the last seat. That is also the practical warning in C1: a
check-then-act booking can pass every test that does not sell an event out under concurrency.

### 2. Pre-created or created? Neither — what matters is whether buyers serialise

The owner's question was pre-created tickets versus tickets created at booking. On these
numbers the answer is that this is the second-order decision. The first-order one is **whether
all buyers of an event have to pass through one row**.

Sell-out race, PostgreSQL, seats sold per second (report, *The sell-out race*):

| Serialises buyers on one row? | Designs | 1k seats | 100k seats |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| no — buyers work on different seat rows | P3 CAS · P2 skip-locked · R3 seat-pool | 6.2k · 3.6k · 3.7k | 6.9k · 3.4k · 3.4k |
| partly — 8 bucket rows | R2 | 904 | 887 |
| yes — one seat at a time or one counter row | P1 · C4 · R1 · P4 · C3 | 1.2k · 854 · 866 · 816 · 953 | 812 · 835 · 868 · 853 · 485 |

The serialising group converges at ~800–900/s regardless of whether its tickets are
pre-created (P1, P4) or created (C4, R1), and regardless of event size. The controlled pair
built for exactly this question agrees: **P4 → C4** (the same counter serialising both,
pre-created vs created ticket) is 842 vs 831/s at 10k seats — no difference beyond noise.

Among designs that do not serialise, pre-creation is what makes it possible: P2 and P3 can hand
different buyers different rows only because the rows exist. R3 shows the rows need not be
tickets: two-column slot tokens plus a created ticket sold at 3.6–4.3k/s, the same as P2
(3.4–3.6k/s), while publishing a 100 000-seat event in 404 ms rather than 602 ms and using
25 MiB rather than 47 MiB of storage.

On YugabyteDB the same structure holds at an absolute level 15–50× lower (1-node: P3
194–400/s; P2 53–93/s; R3 60–86/s; C4, R1, P4, H1 15.5–22/s).

**Recommendation:** for events that can sell out under a crowd, pre-create inventory rows —
narrow ones (R3) if ticket rows are wide — and take a row with SKIP LOCKED or a compare-and-set.
Do not put a counter on the booking path unless its serialisation is acceptable; on this
hardware that ceiling was ~850 sales/s per event on PostgreSQL and ~20/s on YugabyteDB.

> **Note added 2026-09-13, answering the owner's question** *"if the counter has a toll, why
> doesn't it reappear as maintenance of the index P3 uses to find a free seat?"* — The
> counter's toll is **waiting, not work**. The counter update itself executes in 0.046 ms,
> but every buyer of an event updates the *same row*, and a row lock is held until COMMIT, so
> buyers queue for the whole transaction: at C4's ~850 sales/s each sale holds the row about
> 1.2 ms, ten times the work it does on it. P3 does pay index maintenance on every sale, but
> for *different seats* — different index entries, protected by page latches held for
> microseconds rather than locks held to commit — so 32 buyers pay it in parallel. Evidence:
> adding only the counter to otherwise identical P2 cut it 4.3x (3.6k → 842/s); every
> single-row design converges at ~820–900/s whether its tickets are pre-created or created;
> they never retry (1.00 attempts per sale) and leave the CPU mostly idle (26–35% of periods
> throttled, against 79–89% for P2/P3). P3's contention reappears instead as **retries** when
> two buyers pick the same seat — 3.65 attempts per sale on ten-seat events, 1.02 at 100 000.
> Full reasoning, alternatives and the experiment that would confirm it:
> [discussion — counter cost vs index cost](../discussions/counter-cost-vs-index-cost--claude-opus-5--2026-09-13.md).

### 3. Compare-and-set (P3) was fastest everywhere — for a reason worth checking

P3 won every race tier on every topology. Two mechanisms plausibly contribute, and this run
cannot separate them:

- no locking read and no convoy: attempts per sale fell from 3.65 (10 seats) to 1.02 (100 000
  seats) on PostgreSQL — collisions become rare when the random start points spread buyers
  over many free seats;
- **fewer round trips**: P3 issues two autocommit statements per sale; P2, R3, C4 and R1 each
  issue BEGIN, two or three statements and COMMIT. On a CPU-throttled database, round trips
  are work.

The second mechanism is a property of how the harness drives the design, not of CAS itself.
A P2 variant implemented as a single statement (`UPDATE … WHERE ticket_id = (SELECT … FOR
UPDATE SKIP LOCKED) RETURNING`) would test it.

### 4. Hot rows hurt more than sales: the organiser's edit

The race edited the event's description every 20 ms. Wherever the booking transaction updates
the event row, the edit waits in the same queue (report, *Organiser edit p99*):

| | PostgreSQL | YugabyteDB 1-node |
|---|---:|---:|
| C4 counter on the event row | 155–174 ms (1k–100k seats) | 2.0–2.4 s |
| R1 same counter, separate row | 2–14 ms | 23–62 ms |
| P4 counter added to SKIP LOCKED | 180–229 ms | 1.6–2.1 s |
| P2 without counter | 56–59 ms | 88–107 ms |

I expected YugabyteDB's column-level conflict detection to let an update of `description`
coexist with an update of `seats_sold`. It did not, at least with the counter's
`WHERE seats_sold < capacity` read in the same statement. I have not established why.

The C4 → R1 pair shows the extra inventory table buys **nothing for sales** (854 vs 866/s on
PostgreSQL at 1k seats) and **almost everything for everyone else who touches the event row**.
That is a reason to use it, and the report without this experiment would have called the extra
table pointless.

### 5. Designs to avoid, and why

**C5 — `max(seat_no) + 1` with a unique index.** Correct against overbooking, and wrong
another way: after refunds it reaches seat N with holes behind it and answers "sold out" with
seats empty. The churn race caught it on every topology (PostgreSQL 107 seats, YugabyteDB
1-node 148, 3-node 27) and the audit's seat-frontier check flagged exactly those events. It is
also slow for a structural reason: every buyer reaches for the same next number, so ~21–27
attempts per sale at every event size on both engines.

**C2 — SERIALIZABLE check-then-insert.** Correct, and the most expensive way to be correct:
PostgreSQL 116–242 sold/s with 15–23 attempts per sale; it sold 7% of the 100 000-seat event
in 60 s. On YugabyteDB it did not survive the race on either topology. "Just use SERIALIZABLE"
is not a strategy for a hot drop; it can be a strategy for low-contention booking (spread-demand
booking still reached only 105/s on PostgreSQL, though).

**C3 — lock the event, then count.** Correct, but the count sits inside the critical section,
so it slows as the event fills: 943/s at 10 seats down to 485/s at 100 000 on PostgreSQL. It is
C4 with an avoidable O(sold) cost.

### 6. Reservations with expiry

Both hold designs sold at ~500/s on PostgreSQL in the plain race (two transactions per sale).
In the holds experiment, the one decision between them — whether the confirmation checks
`status = 'held' AND expires_at > now()` in the same statement — decided correctness:

| | H0 naive confirm | H1 checked confirm |
|---|---|---|
| PostgreSQL, 1k-seat events | ❌ 5 events +111 seats; 157 confirmed/s | ✅ none; 468 late confirms rejected; 128 confirmed/s |
| YugabyteDB 1-node, 100-seat events | ❌ 3 events +9 | ✅ none; 64 rejected |
| YugabyteDB 3-node, 100-seat events | ❌ 4 events +11 | ✅ none; 60 rejected |

H1's lower confirmed/s most likely reflects the late confirmations it refunds instead of
selling (H0 counts its oversold seats as sales), rather than a cost of the check itself; the run
does not isolate this. The pre-payment check that both designs share was true when it ran; the failure is
entirely in trusting it after the payment call.

### 7. Reads

The availability question separates the families as expected and by a lot. On PostgreSQL,
"seats left" for a 100 000-seat event: counter designs 34–40k ops/s, counting designs
449–1 300 ops/s (P1–P3, R3, C1–C3, C5). "Tickets sold across a band" on YugabyteDB: counter
designs 1.3–2.6k ops/s, P2/P3 29–55 ops/s. So the counter that should stay off the booking path
is still worth having for reads — maintained asynchronously, or kept in its own row (R1) where
it does not block the event row.

### 8. One node or three (YugabyteDB)

The 3-node cluster sold at about the same rate as the single node for every design (e.g. P3
122–402 vs 194–400/s; R2 56–80 vs 45–78/s). I do not read this as "three nodes buy nothing":
every client connected to `yb-n1`, whose SQL layer did all the query and lock work; `yb-n2` and
`yb-n3` logged no deadlock-detector activity during C2's failure while `yb-n1` logged 49 140.
This run measured one query node in front of replicated storage.

## Where I think the measurement is weak

1. **Every database was CPU-bound by its quota.** The report's hazard table shows the
   PostgreSQL container throttled in, for example, 1 133 of 1 275 CFS periods during P2's
   cell and 1 771 of 1 800 during R2's; YugabyteDB cells were throttled for 246–2 198 s each.
   The client was never throttled. Absolute numbers are therefore a 2-CPU database's numbers,
   and **ratios between designs are shaped by CPU cost per sale** as much as by concurrency
   control. A design that spends CPU spinning looks worse here than it would on a larger node.
2. **The harness retries immediately, with no backoff.** This is the doubt I would most like
   resolved. R2 (8 bucket rows, 32 buyers) needed 9–11 attempts per sale on PostgreSQL for
   events of 100 seats and more (24 at ten seats) and saturated the database (1 771/1 800 periods throttled) while R1, a single
   row, mostly waited on locks (468/1 807). R2's failure to beat R1 on PostgreSQL (887 vs
   868/s at 100k) is at least partly the cost of 24 buyers re-asking "is anything unlocked?"
   in a tight loop. The same spin explains P2's 16.6 and P4's 53 attempts per sale on ten-seat
   events. Conclusion 2 is robust to this (P2/P3/R3 barely retry at 1k+ seats); the R1 → R2
   comparison is not.
3. **One trial.** The run's own error bar, from byte-identical reads (C1/C2/C3), was 1.40x worst
   and 1.03x median. The 10k and 100k tiers are two and one events. I have argued only from
   differences of 3x or more, or from correctness outcomes, which do not depend on trials.
4. **Timeouts truncate the large tiers.** With a 60 s race, 9 of 12 PostgreSQL designs and all
   YugabyteDB designs measured only the beginning of the 100 000-seat sale — the emptiest part.
   Designs whose cost grows as the event fills (C3's count, P1's dead index entries) look
   better there than they would over a whole sale.
5. **32 buyers per event, closed loop.** One contention level. Designs ordered by how they
   degrade with more buyers (C5, C2, R2) may reorder at 4 or 128.
6. **The race favours designs with fewer round trips** (conclusion 3); the harness, not the
   design, decides how many statements each booking takes.
7. **YugabyteDB's cluster had a single query node** (conclusion 8), and no real network.
8. **Holds on YugabyteDB ran at `-hold-time-scale 10` and on smaller events** than on
   PostgreSQL; the H0/H1 conclusion is about correctness and holds on both, but confirmed/s is
   not comparable across engines.

## What I would measure next

1. **Backoff variant** of every retrying design (P2, P3, P4, R2, R3, C5): jittered backoff after
   an "all locked" or lost CAS. Re-run the race on PostgreSQL. Expectation to test: R2 overtakes
   R1.
2. **Repeated trials of the race only**, PostgreSQL and YugabyteDB 3-node:
   `./run-study.sh --phases verify,race,churn --extra "-race-trials 3" --tag`.
3. **Buyers sweep** 4 / 16 / 64 / 128 for P2, P3, R2, R3, C4 (`-race-buyers`).
4. **Single-statement SKIP LOCKED** (a P2 variant) to separate round trips from locking in P3's
   lead.
5. **YugabyteDB with connections spread over all three nodes** before any 1-node vs 3-node
   conclusion.
6. **A larger database budget** (4–8 CPUs per node) to see whether the ranking survives when
   the database is not quota-bound — the total-budget question study 01 also left open.
7. **An asynchronous allocator** (requests queued, seats assigned by one worker per event), the
   virtual-waiting-room design none of these 14 represents.
