  axis.** X1 reads 3.0k–6.0k sold/s against P3's 1.1k–4.5k, but P3's three trials

  spreads. The PostgreSQL race comparison is unsettled by these two runs; the
  YugabyteDB one is not.
- **The refund path is where a ledger costs most outside the race.** Cancel throughput on
  `yb-single` falls from 575/s (P3) to 271/s (X1) and on `yb-cluster3` from 404/s to

## Conclusions

**1. The trade is now priced, and the price is on the hot path, not in storage.** Amortised
over a run, X1 costs +33 % storage on PostgreSQL (61.8 MiB against 46.6 MiB in the sibling
run) and one extra table to maintain. What a ticketing operator actually pays is the drop
itself: at 32 buyers on `yb-single` the same crowd sells out at 2.0x the time (10k tier:
459/s → 224/s), and the last "no" a buyer receives is slower too (sold-out-answered p50
1399 ms → 1707 ms; `yb-cluster3` 2001 ms against a tier that timed out). A design decision
that adds one row to the transaction a crowd is contending on multiplies through the
retry loop: attempts-per-sale barely move on PostgreSQL (1.10 → 1.10 at 10k) but p99 sale
latency on `yb-single` goes 200 ms → 453 ms.

**2. The churn race separates the two paths cleanly.** The churn race sells and refunds
concurrently. On `yb-single` P3 holds 150/282/401 sold/s at 10/100/1k seats; X1 reaches
43.9/118/170 — 3.4x, 2.4x, 2.4x. The refund side is the expensive one, consistent with the
isolated-write numbers in the sibling run: X1's cancel is the operation that acquires the
row lock and writes the ledger, and on 3 nodes it drops to 41 % of P3's. If a deployment
runs refunds during a drop, the ledger's cost is not the 2x of the pure race; it is worse.

**3. The unrelated edit is the finding I did not expect.** The race includes an organiser
editing the event page every 20 ms — nothing to do with buying. On PostgreSQL its p99 is
8.6/8.6/9.5 ms for P3 at 1k/10k/100k seats but 51/52/46 ms for X1 (32-buyer run; the
128-buyer run shows the same ordering at 83/87/79 ms against 100/162/178 ms). The ledger
rows are written into the same table space and the same WAL stream as everything else, and
the event row the human edits queues behind them. "One extra insert" is not local to the
sale.

**4. The PostgreSQL result is a measurement, not a design conclusion.** In this run X1
looks 2–3x faster than P3 in the race, which cannot be true of a design that does strictly
more work per sale in the same statement. The tell is in the report's own spread column:
P3's trials disagree by 189 %, 283 %, 159 %, 92 % and 97 % in the race, and by 292 %,
278 % and 141 % in churn, while X1's are 1–47 %. The sibling run (order reversed, 128
buyers, `20260921T205212Z`) reports P3 faster with 4–37 % spreads. Both runs are internally
consistent; they disagree with each other, and the disagreement tracks which design ran
first in the topology. I therefore read the pair as: **YugabyteDB settles the question
(P3 wins the race and churn, X1 wins refund answerability), PostgreSQL needs a design
that interleaves the two designs' trials inside one cell before anyone quotes a ratio.**

**5. The ledger's own audit is the strongest thing in the run.** Every X1 cell, on both
engines, at both buyer counts, reports 0 mismatches and 0 attribution mismatches, at load
and after every race and churn tier — including `yb-cluster3` at 128 buyers, the setting
that produced 246 false attribution problems before AM-04 rewrote the audit to be
order-free. That is what makes the numbers above usable: the sales the ledger records are
the sales that happened, as its own independent check defines them.

## Where I think the measurement is weak

- **No negative control ran in this run.** Only P3 and X1 were measured, so C1 and H0 are
  absent and the report's control line reads "0 of 0 fired". The overbooking audit's
  ability to *detect* overbooking under this run's contention is inherited from study 02's
  published matrix and the AM-02/AM-03 dev checks, not re-demonstrated here. A correct
  design passing is weaker evidence when no wrong design was present to fail.
- **One partial cell.** `yb-cluster3/x1_cas_ledger` has 2-trial medians; the report says so
  and the cell is excluded nowhere, but its ratios are correspondingly softer.
- **PostgreSQL's sign flip between runs is unexplained**, and I have not proven that design
  order is the cause — only that it correlates. The mechanism might instead be the
  interleaved race/churn trial order, or host state (the laptop's other work) during the
  first cell of a topology.
- **Both databases were quota-throttled in this run.** The database container was throttled
  in 638/1000 periods (P3) and 1293/1762 (X1) on `pg-single`, and on `yb-cluster3` in
  7822/29687 and 13168/83280 — the ratios differ between the two designs partly because X1's
  cell takes longer. On a machine where the database is paused for most of a second, a
  throughput ratio is a ratio of how each design uses a throttled core, not of what the
  engines can do; the *ordering* survives that, the absolute numbers do not.
- **The 100k tier times out** on both designs on both YugabyteDB topologies, so those rows
  measure a partial sell-out (31 % and 15 % sold on `yb-single`), not a completed drop.
- **Closed-loop buyers.** The crowd is bounded by the answers it waits for, so these tails
  are a floor; a production drop is open-loop and would show the ledger's queueing worse.
- **One trial per buyer count** (3 trials per tier inside the cell, which is what the
  spreads above are computed over). Design-level repetition across cells is what is missing.

## What I would measure next

1. **Interleave the pair inside one cell**: run P3 and X1 alternately, tier by tier, in a
   single run (or alternate cells P3, X1, P3, X1) so the PostgreSQL comparison has a
   within-run control. Until then, no PostgreSQL ledger-cost ratio should be quoted from
   this study.
2. **Put the controls back in the race run**: add `c1_count_naive` (and `h0_hold_naive` for
   the holds topology) to the design list so the audit is seen to catch overbooking in the
   same contention regime whose correctness is being claimed.
3. **Refund-during-drop**: the churn race cancels 10 %; sweep that to 50 % and 100 % on
   `yb-single` to price the refund path where it is already 2.4x, instead of inferring it
   from an isolated-write cell.
4. **A C4-ledger variant**: X1 = P3 + ledger confounds "answer reports from a ledger" with
   "keep per-seat rows". C4 + `sale_event` separates them.
5. **Open-loop arrivals** using the platform's arrival driver, so the ledger's cost in a
   real drop (where buyers do not wait) is measured rather than bounded.
