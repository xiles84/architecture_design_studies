# Analysis — 20260915T002411Z — claude-opus-5

Companion runs cited here: the repeated race `20260915T173255Z` (`29296b1fe8dea2e3`), and the
two AM-03 repair runs `20260915T232736Z` (`2ccece48793fe693`) and `20260916T000706Z`
(`903de88de503ded2`), which were produced by a later commit than the matrix.

## TL;DR

- **On YugabyteDB, "check the hold in the statement that sells" does not deliver the owner's
  guarantee.** Every correct seat-row design refused holds that were valid: on three nodes,
  S1 41, S2 27, S3 46, K1 28, E1 37, L1 35 in the matrix race. Each refusal was transient —
  the identical statement, re-issued in the same transaction, matched every seat.
- **Retrying a short confirmation once removes them, for free.** S1r recorded **zero** early
  rejections in every run, and all 140 of its retries sold (35 in the matrix, 105 in the
  repeated race). Cluster throughput 132 vs S1's 148 seats/s at 1 000 seats and 129 vs 130 at
  10 000 — inside the 6–15% trial spread. **Use it on YugabyteDB; it costs nothing measurable
  on PostgreSQL.**
- **PostgreSQL never showed the behaviour**: zero early rejections in every correct design,
  in both runs.
- **Layout dominates publishing.** Creating a 100 000-seat event costs 541 ms (PostgreSQL) and
  3 216 ms (YugabyteDB) with per-seat rows, against 0.89 ms and 1.56 ms for L1, which creates
  a claim row only when a seat is held — 600× and 2 000×, with storage 28.9 MiB against 54.9 MiB.
- **Two designs to avoid for hot selling:** a JSONB section document (L2) is correct but pays
  28 976–33 712 compare-and-set retries per race tier on the cluster, with confirm p99 4.2–4.5 s;
  SERIALIZABLE (S4) collapses on YugabyteDB to 0.0–0.1 seats/s with every event timing out.
- **Biggest doubt:** one laptop, database containers CPU-throttled in 63–96% of scheduling
  periods on yb-single. The transient refusals may be a saturation artefact as much as an
  engine property, and their frequency here says nothing about a production cluster.

## What I was looking at

The `small` matrix (41 cells, 14 designs, three topologies), the repeated race (27 cells,
three trials of the 1 000- and 10 000-seat tiers), and two repair runs that recovered what the
matrix lost. Four cells of the matrix failed — S4 and E2 on both YugabyteDB topologies — when a
harness monitor query or a reload's `ANALYZE` timed out on a saturated node; the repair run
re-measured exactly those lifecycles, so no design is missing a phase. I ignored the
100 000-seat race tier on YugabyteDB: calibration showed it sells 2–3% before timing out, so it
was not run there.

Every correctness gate passed (58/58 per cell) and all 15 negative controls fired: S0 stole
holds (16 737 thefts on PostgreSQL's race), K0 sold after expiry (119 late sales), E0's skewed
clock stole valid holds (301 thefts), and E1 with its sweeper stopped left every probed seat
unavailable (59/59, 8/8, 11/11). Without that last one, "no leaked seats everywhere else" would
not have been evidence of anything.

## Conclusions

**Do not trust a single guarded statement on YugabyteDB.** The study's central mechanism —
`UPDATE … WHERE hold_id = $h AND status = 'held' AND hold_expires_at > now()` — silently matched
zero rows for holds that were demonstrably valid: the same transaction's `SELECT` showed the
seats held by that hold, and the identical statement re-issued moments later matched all of
them. It is not an artefact of one design: it appeared in conditional update, lock-then-update,
NOWAIT, payment window, sweeper expiry, claim rows and section-sharded alike, and in the
document design's plain read as well (17 of 17 classified transient in the repair run). It is
therefore best understood as *a read of a recently committed row that does not see it*, not as a
property of any one statement shape. Neither expression pushdown, wait queues nor query-layer
retries explain it (ER-01 diagnosis, `results/devchecks/er01-*`). PostgreSQL, same SQL, same
harness: nothing.

**The defence is a three-line change.** S1r differs from S1 by one decision: when a confirmation
matches fewer seats than the hold, roll back and run it again once, immediately, in a new
transaction. That is safe by construction — the retried statement is still guarded, so it cannot
sell an expired, released or stolen hold — and it worked every time it was needed. If you build
reserved seating on YugabyteDB, write the retry; if you build it on PostgreSQL, the retry is
idle and costs nothing.

**Choose the layout by what you publish, not by what you sell.** Selling throughput barely
separates the seat-row designs (PostgreSQL 1 000-seat race: S1 1.3k, S2 1.3k, K1 1.3k, L1 1.4k
seats/s). Publishing separates them by three orders of magnitude, because pre-creating a row per
seat is the whole cost of an event. If your catalogue is large or events are created often, L1's
claim-on-hold layout is the better trade; its price is that the seat map is derived from claims
rather than read from rows.

**Expiry belongs on the row that is sold.** Moving it to a cart (E2) bought no correctness and
cost heavily: hold throughput fell from 130 to 58 ops/s on PostgreSQL and from 14.4 to 1.2 on
YugabyteDB, and its races ran 2–3.5× slower (PostgreSQL 1 000 seats 605 against 1.3k seats/s;
10 000 seats 168 against 592). Making expiry take effect through a sweeper (E1)
matched S1 while the sweeper ran — and the outage probe shows the price when it stops: seats
whose holds expired stay unavailable, invisible to buyers, until the job comes back.

**K1's payment window is worth it for this scenario.** Extending a valid hold once at checkout
removed most late refusals (45 → 8 in PostgreSQL's 1 000-seat lifecycle) with no throughput cost
inside trial spread (141 vs 148 seats/s on the cluster). For "select seats, then pay", that is
the difference between a refund and a sale.

## Where I think the measurement is weak

- **One machine, CPU-starved.** Database containers were throttled in 63–96% of scheduling
  periods per cell on yb-single; on the cluster's nodes, 7–56% in the two cells whose
  `cpu.stat` I read while diagnosing failures. Both S4's collapse and the transient
  refusals are consistent with saturation; a cluster with headroom may show neither at this rate.
- **The matrix is one trial per cell.** The repeated race gives the error bar: spreads of 3–34%,
  and 34% at the 10 000-seat tier means a 1.3× difference there is not a difference.
- **No real network.** Consensus round trips cost microseconds between containers, so the
  three-node numbers understate replication and overstate the cluster's attractiveness.
- **Compressed lifecycle time** (one human minute = 50 ms on PostgreSQL, 500 ms on YugabyteDB)
  makes database latency a much larger share of a hold than it would be against a real 40 minutes.
- **L2's matrix refusals are unclassified** (the diagnostic did not exist then); only the repair
  run's 17 are classified. Its race numbers come from two commits, and I have not merged them.
- **L3 is unexplained.** It is 4–8× slower than S1 on YugabyteDB under contention, yet its
  single-statement plan is indistinguishable (1 storage read request, 7.6 ms against 3.8 ms).
  I do not know whether hash-sharding by section hurts the block update, the seat-map read, or
  the tablet balance, and I am not willing to guess from these runs.

## What I would measure next

1. **Put a number on the visibility gap.** A probe that commits a hold, then confirms after a
   controlled delay (1, 5, 20, 100, 500 ms), on an idle node and a loaded one. The study only
   knows the gap is 20–100 ms wide when it happens; it does not know its distribution.
2. **Separate saturation from engine behaviour:** the same race with the database given 4 and 6
   CPUs. If transient refusals vanish with headroom, they are a capacity story.
3. **L3 under concurrency with `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, DIST)` captured during the race**, not before
   it, plus tablet-level metrics — the one design whose cost this study cannot attribute.
4. **S1r on PostgreSQL at 64 buyers**, to confirm the retry stays free where it is never needed.
5. **An E1 outage sweep** (5, 15, 45 human minutes) to price the sweeper's availability risk.
